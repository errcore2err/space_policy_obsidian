The memo records the development, planning, and policy rationale for a U.S. scientific‑satellite program that was being coordinated by the National Security Council (NSC) in the mid‑1950s. Its main points are:

1. **Purpose and Scope** 
    - The program was conceived as a _scientific_ effort, not a military one, intended to launch small scientific satellites in 1957–1958 to test rocketry, instrumentation, and ground‑station operations, followed by a fully instrumented satellite in early 1958【1】.
    - The objective was to collect data for the International Geophysical Year (IGY, July 1957–December 1958) and to demonstrate U.S. capability in space without compromising classified launch technology【1】.
2. **Presidential and Inter‑agency Backing** 
    - President Eisenhower publicly endorsed the program at a press conference on 9 Oct 1957, emphasizing the importance of adhering to the U.S. scientific‑satellite plan and framing it as a well‑reasoned, deliberately planned national effort【1】.
    - The memo notes coordination among the President’s office, the National Academy of Sciences, the National Science Foundation, and the Department of Defense, with the latter tasked to develop a small satellite launch capability by 1958 without prejudice to larger defense projects【1】.
3. **Technical and Safety Considerations** 
    - Satellites were to be designed so that, upon re‑entry, they would largely vaporize, minimizing hazards to the ground【1】.
    - Both instrumented and uninstrumented versions were planned, the latter serving as test vehicles for launch systems and tracking infrastructure【1】.
4. **Policy Constraints** 
    - The program was required not to delay existing U.S. satellite or related R&D work, to protect classified launch information, and to avoid obligating prior consent from any nation over which the satellite might pass—preserving “Freedom of Space”【1】.
5. **International Context and Competition** 
    - The memo anticipates a possible Soviet announcement of a satellite launch and stresses the need for a coordinated U.S. response, warning against Communist propaganda that could portray the project as “evil” or threatening【1】.
    - It highlights the U.S. commitment to the IGY, noting that the National Committee for the IGY had requested government support for a scientific satellite that could dovetail with worldwide geophysical observations【1】.
6. **Budget and Planning** 
    - A “Financial Appendix” and “Technical Annex” (Annex A, Annex B) were attached, outlining costs and the technical rationale for the program【1】.
    - The NSC planning board recommended initiating the Defense Department’s small‑satellite capability promptly, while also continuing larger surveillance‑satellite studies for future use【1】.

**Broader Context of the U.S. Space Program (mid‑1950s)**

- **Cold‑War Competition** – After the Soviet Union’s launch of _Sputnik 1_ on 4 Oct 1957, the United States was under intense pressure to demonstrate comparable technical prowess. The memo’s references to a Soviet satellite and the need to “congratulate Soviet achievements” reflect the emerging space race atmosphere【1】.
    
- **International Geophysical Year (IGY)** – The IGY (July 1957–December 1958) was a worldwide scientific initiative to study the Earth’s atmosphere, magnetic field, and related phenomena. Both the United States and the Soviet Union saw the IGY as an opportunity to place scientific payloads in orbit, making satellite development a diplomatic as well as scientific priority【1】.
    
- **Early U.S. Space Policy** – Prior to the creation of NASA (established in 1958), space activities were scattered among military services, the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA), and civilian scientific agencies. NSC 5520 represents an early attempt to centralize policy and secure inter‑agency coordination for a civilian‑led scientific satellite effort.
    
- **Technological Foundations** – By 1955 the U.S. had conducted several high‑altitude sounding‑rocket flights and was developing the Vanguard and Atlas launch vehicles. The memo’s call for “preliminary design studies and initial critical component development” aimed to leverage these emerging capabilities for a rapid 1957‑58 launch schedule【1】.
    
- **Policy Emphasis on “Freedom of Space”** – The document stresses that U.S. actions should not require prior consent from other nations, a principle that would later be codified in the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. This reflects early awareness of the need for an open, non‑appropriated space environment【1】.
    

**Conclusion**

NSC 5520 outlines a coordinated, scientifically driven U.S. satellite program launched in response to the IGY and the Soviet space achievement. It emphasizes rapid development, safety, inter‑agency cooperation, and a policy posture that preserves freedom of space while avoiding escalation. The memo captures a pivotal moment when the United States moved from experimental rockets to an organized national effort that would soon evolve into the NASA‑led space program.
