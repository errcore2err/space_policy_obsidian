- Author proposes that we are now between the first and second stages in the space age
	- First began with Sputnik, and was focused on initial departure from Earth, and ended with Challenger

- In that period, author argues we went through a romantic period of space exploration, starting with sputnik and Gagarin "shocking the imagination"
	- Introduced a "climate of opinion", similar to the invention of the steam engine or electricity 
	- Utopianism is a concomitant of technological breakthrough, and he argues that something similar happened with the space age 
		- "Was both an exaggeration and underestimation of what ultimately came to pass"
		- Instead of flying cars, cities, etc., the real future of aviation was to be found not at things like flashy air shows, but rather manufacturing plants or government research labs

- Draws parallels between the first manned spaceflights and the Wright brothers flights (or at least early in the ages of aviation)
	- Flyers themselves are almost as important as the technologies that enabled their feats (at least to the public)
	- Both had military and civil applications, but was unclear how exactly those would come about
	- "War and commerce shaped the future of aviation. So too will they shape the future of spaceflight"
		- However, despite programs like star wars [[SDI]] which are on the horizon at the time, it's unclear weather space will get the required boost in resources and commitments to drive space exploitation, and similar arguments can be made on the commercial side 

- Argues that it's unclear when the next transformation to the next space age might be completed 
	- Change being delayed by current national policies intent upon extending the romantic era of spaceflight and building the entire future of space around a program of barnstorming, "deferring mature exploitation of space in a childish infatuation with circus", and I take this to refer to the sort of tit for tat strategy that the US and Russia were doing at the time 
		- It was predicted in 1969 that "the pressure on Congress to reduce the space budget still further will increase unless the orientation of NASA is based less on space spectaculars and more on the production of tangible and economic benefits."

- In 1969, there was an opportunity for NASA to map out a new future for the agency, Nixon formed a group which suggested Mars and a space station, whose main purpose was to help get to mars
	- Space science played a role, but the most important goal was the develop "new challenges for man in space"
	- Echoed the NASA philosophy of the 1960s, making manned spaceflight an end in itself and proposed a series of logical steps as the centerpiece of the civilian space program
