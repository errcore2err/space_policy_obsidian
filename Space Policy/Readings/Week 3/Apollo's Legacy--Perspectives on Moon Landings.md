This document contains two sections from Roger Launius's _Apollo's Legacy: Perspectives on the Moon Landings_ (Smithsonian Books, 2019): Chapter 1, "Versions of Reality," and the concluding chapter, "Remembering Apollo."

## Chapter 1: Versions of Reality

Launius opens with the story of Apollo 12 astronaut Pete Conrad, whose folksy, unscripted quip upon stepping onto the Moon (referencing his height relative to Neil Armstrong) contrasted sharply with the more solemn public image of the first landing — an anecdote he uses to illustrate how differently Apollo has been perceived and remembered. He pairs this with an old baseball joke about three umpires describing how they call balls and strikes, which he uses as a framework for three philosophical stances toward historical reality: an absolutist view (reality exists independent of interpretation), a modern/rationalist view (reality is shaped by perception), and a postmodern view (an event's meaning is constructed by whoever interprets it). He argues the postmodern framing increasingly dominates how Americans discuss the Moon landings.

Launius then lays out competing narratives of Apollo:

**The triumphalist narrative** treats Apollo as proof of American exceptionalism — a demonstration of what the US can accomplish, reinforced by Cold War competition with the Soviet Union. He cites Lyndon Johnson's 1965 remark that America's space successes made other problems seem less daunting, and traces the "if we can put a man on the Moon, why can't we do X" catchphrase. This narrative also served foreign policy purposes, with the Moon landing generating congratulations from nearly every nation with US diplomatic relations, reinforcing American technological prestige during the Cold War.

**The "moondoggle" critique** from the political left held Apollo was wasteful spending that could have gone to social programs. Vannevar Bush and sociologist Amitai Etzioni both criticized the cost-benefit tradeoff in the early 1960s, and Democratic senators like Fulbright, Mondale, and Proxmire pushed back against Apollo funding in favor of Great Society priorities. Launius notes that, contrary to popular memory, more than half of Americans favored the lunar program at only one point before Apollo 11 (October 1965) — polls consistently showed Americans ranked spaceflight among programs they'd most like to see cut.

**The libertarian/conservative critique** held that an activist federal government shouldn't be undertaking projects like Apollo at all. Eisenhower himself questioned the rush to the Moon in a 1962 article, and Barry Goldwater called for redirecting funds to defense. This criticism went dormant after Apollo's success but resurfaced in the 1980s as conservatives reassessed the 1960s; historian Walter McDougall's work is discussed at length as rehabilitating Eisenhower while criticizing Apollo as fostering an unhealthy "technocracy" — a permanent alliance between government and technological development that McDougall saw as corrosive to American individual liberty.

**Moon landing denial/conspiracy theories** form a fourth strand, which Launius connects to a decline in institutional trust (citing a December 2017 poll showing only 18% of Americans trusted government to do right "always" or "most of the time") and situates within a broader postmodern tendency to treat historical narratives as constructed rather than factual — drawing a parallel to JFK assassination conspiracy theories.

The chapter closes by returning to the umpire metaphor, framing the entirety of Apollo's historical memory as a contest between these four "stories" — essentially a battle for control of national memory.

## Conclusion: Remembering Apollo

Launius argues that Apollo's popular memory functions as a national myth — not in the sense of falsehood, but as a culturally meaningful story that, per historian Alex Roland (quoted at length), serves as a kind of "tribal ritual" retelling rather than strict history, hitting familiar narrative beats: Kennedy's visionary decision, the workforce that built the hardware, the 1967 Apollo 1 fire, Apollo 8's Earthrise photo, Armstrong's landing, Apollo 13's crisis, and the anticlimactic final missions.

He then examines the role of **prestige** as a motivator, tracing it back to a prescient 1946 RAND Corporation study that predicted orbital satellite capability would carry geopolitical weight comparable to the atomic bomb. He links this to Joseph Nye's concept of "soft power" — influence through attraction and demonstration rather than coercion — arguing Apollo functioned as a soft-power instrument, especially after the Sputnik shock of 1957–58 reframed spaceflight as a matter of Cold War prestige. He cites Kennedy's and Johnson's rhetoric about needing to lead in space, and Gallup polling showing world opinion shifted from seeing the Soviets as the space leader to seeing the US that way by the height of Apollo.

Launius also discusses **national pride** as a distinct motivator from international prestige — the idea that Apollo built domestic confidence in government and technology, citing journalist Gregg Easterbrook's description of spaceflight as a "metaphor of national inspiration." He connects this to Americans' emotional attachment to spaceflight symbolism (astronaut imagery ranking near the flag and bald eagle as patriotic symbols) and the outsized public reaction to failures like the Challenger/Columbia disasters or the Hubble mirror flaw.

A third theme is **progress and American exceptionalism**, tied to Tocqueville's observation of Americans' faith in human perfectibility and historian Richard Hughes's concept of America as a "millennial nation." Launius notes Carl Sagan's reflections on Apollo fostering a sense of planetary unity, while also flagging the tension between this progress narrative and a "myth of innocence" that can be used to justify exporting American values regardless of consequences elsewhere. He includes Sam Kinison's comedic jingoistic riff on other nations "impressing" the US as an illustration of this nationalist undercurrent.

The chapter then traces the political afterlife of Apollo through subsequent failed attempts to replicate it:

- Nixon's 1969 Space Task Group recommended an ambitious post-Apollo program (space station, shuttle, Moon base, Mars mission), which Nixon largely rejected, approving only an Earth-orbital Space Shuttle.
- George H.W. Bush's 1989 Space Exploration Initiative proposed a $400 billion (possibly up to $1 trillion) return to the Moon and eventual Mars mission; Congress essentially zeroed out its funding.
- George W. Bush's 2004 "Vision for Space Exploration" set similarly ambitious goals; it lost momentum and was cancelled by the Obama administration in 2010.
- Trump's December 2017 directive to return astronauts to the Moon (with Mars beyond) is noted as the most recent iteration, with Launius observing that follow-through investment remained modest as of the book's writing.

Launius concludes that this repeated pattern — bold announcement, insufficient funding, quiet abandonment — reflects the unique and likely unrepeatable convergence of political, technological, and Cold War circumstances that made the original Apollo program possible. He cites a 1999 poll of opinion leaders ranking the Moon landing as the second most significant news event of the 20th century (behind splitting the atom), including commentary from historian Arthur Schlesinger Jr. on its long-term significance. He closes by comparing Apollo's legacy to World War II's "greatest generation" — likely to be permanently venerated as a singular achievement — while suggesting the broader vision of continued human space exploration and colonization that inspired Apollo has largely faded, and that any revival of ambition would probably require a new discovery of concrete value on the Moon itself.
